## Question and fixed intervention

Under CUBIC and ordinary `tcp_slow_start_after_idle=1`, does granting a demand-backed stream up to 4 MiB of already reserved receive-pool credit improve the complete fixed workload? This tests an initial application-credit ramp. It does not reproduce retained pre-cwnd, reset ssthresh or test the namespace intervention again. Slow controls already contain substantial unsent TCP data, so failure to improve is informative and must not prompt adaptive cap/trigger tuning within the screen.

For each aggregate `GrowingConn` receive-wrapper lifetime, count only successful reads under the current `n > 0 && err == nil` semantics. On the first crossing of **512 KiB cumulative consumption**, make exactly one early-share attempt. Do not mutate constructors or treat HTTP headers/methods as eligibility. Do not reset the attempt/counter on idle, direction changes or request boundaries; wrappers are directional and reused logical streams retain their receive state. A no-op consumes the attempt too, with normal demand growth continuing afterward.

Under the existing wrapper/growth-owner lock order, choose target as allocation-unit round-down of the minimum of **4 MiB**, current already-backed session receive limit divided by four, negotiated stream limits and operator maximum. Publish only an increase. The helper must have no session-reservation, slab-allocation or session-growth branch. Retain ownership checks, closed-state handling, allocation-before-byte publication and ambiguous/failure semantics. Refresh current credit before continuing the ordinary estimator; do not reset, pre-seed or fabricate demand history.

A fixed 4 MiB ceiling avoids handing out an arbitrarily large quarter of a 1 GiB pool based on half a megabyte of consumption. A quarter-window recycling calculation leaves about 3 MiB at normal update eligibility, above the nominal 29.7 MB/s × 80 ms flight demand. This is a sizing rationale, not a rate guarantee. The trigger is evidence of consumption, not proof of benign behavior or HTTP bulk traffic: a hostile reader can consume 512 KiB then stall.
